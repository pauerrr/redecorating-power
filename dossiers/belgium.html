This dossier follows a strict legal-inclusion rule: named individuals are identified only where there is a final criminal conviction, a formally accepted plea deal with admitted guilt, or a documented resignation over a scandal. 1. Country snapshot

  • Corruption Perceptions Index (Transparency International): Belgium scored 69/100 in the 2025 CPI, ranking 21st of 182 countries — statistically unchanged from 2024 (69/100, 22nd of 180), but down sharply from 77/100 in 2016. Transparency International Belgium describes the country as "stagnating" and warns that experts and the business community increasingly believe corruption is rising with no effective policy response. Transparency.org — CPI 2025 · Transparency International Belgium, "CPI 2025: Belgium stagnates" · Transparency.org — CPI 2024 Belgium · Brussels Times, on the 2024 six-place drop

  • Public perception (Eurobarometer): In the European Commission's Special Eurobarometer surveys on corruption (2023/2024 waves), 62% of Belgians say corruption is widespread in their country — high in absolute terms, though below the EU average of around 70%. European Commission — Eurobarometer on corruption, 2024 wave · 2023 wave

  • GRECO (Council of Europe Group of States against Corruption): In its Fifth Evaluation Round, Second Compliance Report on Belgium (adopted 18–22 March 2024, published 7 May 2024), GRECO found that of 22 anti-corruption recommendations concerning members of parliament, ministers, and law enforcement, only 2 had been fully implemented, 2 partly implemented, and 18 remained not implemented — one of the weaker compliance records GRECO has issued for a Western European state. It specifically flagged Belgium's slow progress on parliamentary codes of conduct, asset-declaration transparency, and lobbying regulation, an especially sharp criticism given that Belgium hosts the EU institutions at the center of the Qatargate scandal (see below). Belgium did adopt a Code of Conduct for Ministers, effective 27 June 2023, covering conflicts of interest, gifts, and outside offices. GRECO, Second Compliance Report, GrecoRC5(2024)3

  • OECD Working Group on Bribery — Phase 4 report on Belgium (adopted 11–14 March 2025): found that Belgium had concluded only 3 foreign-bribery cases since its previous 2013 review, resulting in 5 individuals convicted and zero companies sanctioned. The OECD criticized weak proactive investigation, under-resourcing of enforcement at every stage, and the absence of incentives for corporate compliance or voluntary disclosure, while welcoming a 2024 law extending the statute of limitations for bribery offenses. OECD press release, March 2025

  • Cost of corruption: No solid, methodologically transparent estimate of the economy-wide cost of corruption specific to Belgium could be verified. A frequently circulated figure of roughly €4 billion lost annually in Belgium's public procurement sector traces to a single secondary source without a clearly documented methodology and should be treated as unverified rather than cited as fact. DWIRE — "The High Cost of Corruption in Belgium's Public Procurement System" (unverified methodology). By contrast, EU-wide (not Belgium-specific) modeling by RAND Europe has put the cost of corruption across the whole EU at up to €990 billion per year. Absent a credible Belgium-specific figure, this dossier does not present one as fact.

  • Institutional trust: In the OECD's 2024 Survey on Drivers of Trust in Public Institutions, Belgians reported only 42% trust in the federal parliament and just 29% trust in political parties — trust in the federal government fell from 47% (2023) to 41% (2025) after a partial post-pandemic recovery. Courts and the judiciary stood at 53%. OECD — Trust Survey, Belgium country note

2. Major cases

2.1 The Agusta–Dassault affair (1988 bribes, tried and decided 1998)

Institution: Belgian Ministry of Defence procurement; the Socialist Party machine (Flemish SP and Francophone PS). Mechanism: In 1988, Italian helicopter manufacturer Agusta and French aerospace firm Dassault paid roughly 110 million Belgian francs (about $3.2 million at the time) in bribes, channeled through shell accounts, to Belgian socialist parties in exchange for Belgian Armed Forces contracts (helicopter and related defense procurement). Because several of those implicated held ministerial rank, the case could only be tried by Belgium's Court of Cassation, sitting in a special constitutional procedure reserved for former ministers, with a 15-judge panel. The trial opened in August 1998 and the verdict was delivered on 23 December 1998.

Convicted, final judgment, 23 December 1998, Court of Cassation: - Willy Claes — Belgian Economy Minister at the time of the bribes, later NATO Secretary-General (from October 1994). He resigned as NATO chief on 20 October 1995 once the affair became public and Belgium's Chamber moved to lift his immunity, declaring himself "totally innocent." Convicted of passive corruption; sentenced to a three-year suspended prison term and a five-year ban on holding public office. Wikipedia — Agusta scandal · Wikipedia — Willy Claes - Guy Coëme — former Defence Minister and Deputy Prime Minister (PS), who had already resigned from government in April 1996 over the affair. Convicted of passive corruption; sentenced to a two-year suspended prison term, a 60,000-franc fine, and a five-year ban on public office. La Libre, retrospective on the Agusta-Dassault fallout - Guy Spitaels — former Minister-President of Wallonia (PS), who had resigned in 1994 as the scandal emerged. Convicted of passive corruption; two-year suspended sentence, fined 60,000 francs, five-year ban on public office. Wikipedia — Guy Spitaels - Serge Dassault — French businessman, Dassault company. Convicted of active bribery; 18-month suspended sentence. The Irish Times, "Belgian court finds Claes, 10 officials and French businessman guilty of corruption" - Johan Delanghe — Claes's cabinet secretary. Convicted of influence peddling in both the Agusta and Dassault strands; 18-month suspended sentence. - Alfons Puelinckx — lawyer and financial intermediary who set up the shell accounts used to funnel the bribes. Convicted; two-year suspended sentence. - Guy Mathot, a further former minister tried alongside the others, was acquitted — he is excluded from any framing of guilt.

Web of connections: the affair implicated the top leadership of Belgium's Francophone and Flemish socialist parties simultaneously, and forced the resignation of a sitting NATO Secretary-General — a first for the alliance. It remains the country's paradigmatic "grand corruption" case and the reason Belgium created a special procedure for prosecuting former ministers.

Aftermath: Willy Claes went on to further public roles despite the conviction, later giving interviews maintaining his version of events. In a December 2023 Flemish television interview he stated (in Dutch): "Ik ben ten onrechte veroordeeld" ("I was wrongfully convicted") and "Er zijn miljoenen naar de partij gegaan, maar ik zat er niet tussen en wist niets van de constructie" ("Millions went to the party, but I wasn't involved and knew nothing of the arrangement"). VRT NWS, "De Afspraak," 11 December 2023

Sources: Wikipedia — Agusta scandal · Encyclopédie Universalis, "Décision judiciaire dans l'affaire Agusta-Dassault, 23 décembre 1998" · EBSCO Research Starters, "Prominent Belgians Are Sentenced in Agusta-Dassault Corruption Scandal" · Wikipédia FR — Affaire Agusta-Dassault

2.2 Qatargate — the European Parliament cash-for-influence scandal (broke December 2022; ongoing as of 2026)

Institution: The European Parliament, investigated and prosecuted by Belgian federal authorities because the alleged acts occurred largely in Brussels. Mechanism: Belgian police, acting on an intelligence-led investigation, arrested several people on 9 December 2022 on suspicion of a scheme in which Qatar (and, in a parallel strand, Morocco) allegedly paid cash and gifts to current and former MEPs and their associates in exchange for favorable action and statements inside the European Parliament. Searches recovered roughly €1.5 million in cash from several addresses, including bags of banknotes found in the Brussels hotel room and home of former MEP Antonio Panzeri.

Resolved legal status (plea deal): - Antonio Panzeri, former Italian MEP (S&D) and head of an NGO, "Fight Impunity," at the center of the network — reached a formal cooperation/"repentance" agreement with Belgian prosecutors on 17 January 2023, only the second use of this procedure in Belgian legal history. He admitted guilt, agreed to name other suspects and provide evidence, and accepted a sentence of one year in prison plus a fine and forfeiture of roughly €1 million in assets. He was later moved from prison to electronic-monitored house arrest, with those restrictions lifted in September 2023. His lawyer, Laurent Kennes, stated on the day of the deal: "Mr Panzeri confesses today to having actively participated in acts of corruption in connection with Qatar and in connection with Morocco and therefore to having been corrupted and to having corrupted others." Washington Post, 17 January 2023 · Euronews, 18 January 2023 · Wikipedia — Antonio Panzeri

Resigned / removed from office, not convicted: - Eva Kaili, Greek MEP, was formally removed as a Vice-President of the European Parliament by a plenary vote on 13 December 2022 (625 in favor, 1 against, 2 abstentions), under Rule 21 of Parliament's Rules of Procedure — a removal from office over the scandal, not a criminal conviction. She was held in pre-trial detention until mid-April 2023, then under house arrest with an electronic tag until 25 May 2023. She denies all criminal charges and her case remains ongoing (see below); she is included here only for the fact of her removal from the Vice-Presidency, not as convicted of any crime. European Parliament press release, 13 December 2022

Proceedings ongoing — not included by name as convicted (as of research date, mid-2026): The great majority of individuals implicated in Qatargate have not been convicted and have not entered plea deals; Belgian prosecutors requested trial referrals but, as of the most recent reporting found (through February–April 2026), no trial date had been set and the case remained at the pre-trial/investigative-chambers stage, dominated by procedural disputes over the legality of the investigation. In February 2026 the Brussels Court of Appeal rejected defense arguments that the probe was fundamentally flawed, clearing the way for the case to continue — still without a trial date. Accordingly, this dossier names the following individuals only by role, not as guilty of any offense: - Eva Kaili (Greek MEP) — separately from the Vice-Presidency removal noted above, she remains formally charged with participation in a criminal organization, corruption, and money laundering, and denies all charges. A related, distinct allegation concerning misuse of parliamentary assistant expenses led to a further immunity-waiver vote in February 2024. Proceedings ongoing; not resolved by conviction or plea deal. - Francesco Giorgi (parliamentary assistant, partner of Eva Kaili) — released to electronic monitoring in February 2023 after agreeing to cooperate with investigators; remains formally charged. Proceedings ongoing. - Marc Tarabella (former Belgian MEP, PS) — charged with corruption and money laundering; denies wrongdoing. Did not stand for re-election in the June 2024 European elections. Proceedings ongoing. - Andrea Cozzolino (former Italian MEP) — charged; denies wrongdoing; as of December 2025 his defense was litigating alleged irregularities in the investigation before the Brussels Court of Appeal. Proceedings ongoing. - Niccolò Figà-Talamanca (Secretary-General of the NGO "No Peace Without Justice") — released without ongoing detention in February 2023 but remains formally implicated; publicly disputes the case. Proceedings ongoing. - Maria Arena (former Belgian MEP, PS) — formally charged in January 2025 with membership in a criminal organization (a charge her representatives say excludes corruption or money laundering); she denies the charges and did not stand for re-election in 2024. Proceedings ongoing. - A former senior trade-union official was briefly detained in December 2022 and publicly acknowledged receiving a payment from Panzeri's NGO, which he described as a legitimate donation; he was released without charge and is not part of the ongoing prosecution.

None of these individuals are named in this dossier's People Index as "convicted" — their status is "proceedings ongoing," explicitly not equivalent to guilt.

Aftermath: Qatargate triggered a September 2023 European Parliament vote to tighten its own conduct rules — introducing a cooling-off period for former MEPs before they may lobby Parliament, mandatory Transparency Register registration for organizers of interest-representation events, and mandatory declaration of meetings with third-country diplomats. Transparency International EU and Corporate Europe Observatory have criticized the reforms as leaving loopholes, including a narrow definition of "lobbyist" that excludes many in-house corporate representatives. Transparency International EU, "Three years since Qatargate" · Corporate Europe Observatory, December 2023 · Wikipedia — Qatar corruption scandal at the European Parliament

Sources: European Parliament press release, 13 December 2022 · Washington Post, 17 January 2023 · Balkan Insight, 6 February 2024 · Euronews, 20 January 2025, on Maria Arena · Wikipedia — Qatar corruption scandal at the European Parliament

2.3 The Publifin "sector committees" affair (2013–2017)

Institution: Publifin, a Liège-region intermunicipal utility (electricity, gas, and telecom distribution), and its commercial arm Nethys. Mechanism: From mid-2013, Publifin ran three "sector committees" (Energy, Telecom, and "Liège-Ville"), comprising roughly 25–26 politicians from the PS, MR, and cdH parties, who were paid gross monthly attendance fees of €1,340 to €2,871 regardless of how much work — or how many meetings — they actually attended. The scandal broke on 20 December 2016 after Liège alderman Cédric Halin leaked the payment schedules to the press. Examples documented by investigators: MEP Anne Delvaux (cdH) received €22,785 despite attending zero meetings between October 2013 and June 2016; Alain Defays (cdH) received €67,198 for attending four meetings. The three committees cost at least €1,998,985 between mid-2013 and December 2016. Publifin abolished the committees on 22 December 2016.

Resigned, not convicted: - Paul Furlan, Walloon Minister for Local Authorities (PS), responsible for regulatory oversight of intermunicipal companies like Publifin — resigned on 26 January 2017. - André Gilles, PS provincial deputy and president of Publifin and of the Liège provincial college — announced his resignation on 9 March 2017, effective end of that month; he was subsequently expelled from the PS on 20 April 2017. - Stéphane Moreau, PS mayor of the municipality of Ans and simultaneously CEO of Nethys/Publifin — left the PS party in spring 2017 amid expulsion proceedings, then resigned as mayor of Ans in the following months (succeeded by Grégory Philippin). He remained Nethys's CEO until a separate, later scandal (see Nethysgate, below).

Legal proceedings and outcome: A Walloon parliamentary inquiry commission (report of 6 July 2017) referred 15 sets of potential criminal violations to prosecutors. Seven former Publifin board members — Georges Pire, André Denis, Jean-Marie Gillon, Robert Botterman, Pol Guillaume, Pierre Stassart, and Catherine Maes — were tried for conflict of interest and acquitted in December 2019; the acquittals were upheld on appeal on 9 September 2021 and a further appeal to the Court of Cassation was rejected, making the acquittals final. No one was ultimately convicted in the criminal proceedings arising from Publifin.

Sources: Wikipédia FR — Affaire Publifin · RTBF, on the final acquittals · La Libre, on the individual payment amounts · RTBF, on Moreau's resignation as mayor of Ans

2.4 The Samusocial scandal (2017; referred to trial in 2026)

Institution: Samusocial, a Brussels nonprofit providing emergency shelter to homeless people, and the Brussels CPAS (public social welfare center). Mechanism: Board members of Samusocial received €140 per meeting in attendance fees, with annual payment pools of €56,000 (2015) and €59,920 (2016) — roughly two-thirds of which went to just two people. Cumulative totals over longer windows range from about €61,000 each (2008–2013) to €112,980–113,400 each (2008–2016), depending on the period counted; the 2026 criminal referral specifically cites around €51,000 gross each for 2014–2016. A 2018 parliamentary inquiry found €346,300 in total reimbursements it deemed improperly justified, and investigators could not locate meeting minutes for some paid sessions. Separately, Pascale Peraïta, as Samusocial's administratrice déléguée, earned a €192,705 annual salary plus a €33,000 irregular-hours bonus on top of her board fees. The scandal broke on 31 May 2017 via revelations from Brussels regional MP Alain Maron.

Resigned, not convicted: - Yvan Mayeur, Mayor of Brussels (PS) and a Samusocial board member — resigned as mayor on 8 June 2017 under intense political pressure, succeeded by Philippe Close. He left the PS party on 30 June 2017, saying: "J'ai probablement commis des erreurs, je le regrette profondément" ("I probably made mistakes, I deeply regret it"). He resigned his remaining Brussels city council seat effective 25 September 2017, opening his resignation letter with "Mais, qu'est-ce que j'ai fait?" ("But, what have I done?") and closing with "Je pars la tête haute" ("I'm leaving with my head held high"). La Libre, 30 June 2017 · BX1, resignation letter, 21 September 2017 - Pascale Peraïta, President of the Brussels CPAS and Samusocial's managing director — resigned as CPAS president on the same day as Mayeur, 8 June 2017.

Legal proceedings, current status: After a nine-year investigation, Brussels' chambre du conseil ruled on 27 February 2026 to refer Mayeur and Peraïta — along with Michel Degueldre (former Samusocial board president), Rita Glineur (former CPAS secretary-general), and a former chief of staff of Peraïta — to criminal trial on charges including embezzlement by a public officeholder, accounting fraud, and abuse of corporate assets. No trial date or verdict has been set as of this writing; none of the five has been convicted. They are named here only for their documented resignations (Mayeur, Peraïta) and are otherwise described by role, not guilt.

Sources: RTBF, "5 inculpés... renvoyés," 27 February 2026 · La Libre, 27 February 2026 · Wikipédia FR — Affaire du Samusocial · La DH, Mayeur's resignation as mayor, 8 June 2017

2.5 Nethys / "Nethysgate" — the golden parachutes scandal (2019–ongoing)

Institution: Nethys, the Liège-region holding company descended from Publifin, and its board's Nomination and Remuneration Committee (CNR). Mechanism: In May 2018, Nethys's CNR approved "retention bonuses" for senior executives — intended to offset a new Walloon public-sector pay cap — mostly paid out from October 2019, totaling more than €18.6 million gross across four executives. Reported figures for CEO Stéphane Moreau's individual share vary by source and accounting scope, from about €5.8 million in early press leaks to as much as €11.6 million in the prosecution's later framing; CFO Pol Heyse and Bénédicte Bayer reportedly shared a further roughly €12 million between them, with Diego Aquilina also among the four beneficiaries. Courts subsequently froze €15.5 million in disputed payments. A December 2020 Deloitte forensic audit additionally uncovered a secret bank account used by Moreau and Heyse, through which some €38.7 million in "atypical expenses" flowed across Nethys and affiliated entities (Voo, Win, Elicio, L'Avenir) in 2017–2019. The scandal was triggered by an October 2019 media investigation into undisclosed asset sales, which the Walloon regional government annulled on 6 October 2019; it separately annulled the CNR's bonus commitments on 13 December 2019.

Resigned / dismissed, not convicted: - Stéphane Moreau — dismissed as Nethys CEO "immediately and without indemnity" by the Nethys board in October 2019 (a firing, announced by board president Pierre Meyers, distinct from his earlier, separate resignation as mayor of Ans in the Publifin affair). Upon a later arrest in January 2021, his lawyer stated that Moreau "nie avec force toute infraction" ("denies with force any infraction") and was "consterné par les reproches qui lui sont formulés" ("dismayed by the accusations made against him"). La DH, 24 January 2021

Proceedings ongoing — not included by name as convicted: Liège prosecutors filed formal trial-referral requests on 11 December 2025 for ten suspects, and a broader request on 28 February 2026 sought referral of 22 people across all Nethys sub-dossiers, including Moreau, while requesting a dismissal ("non-lieu") for a former Walloon minister. A separate Congo/DRC-related sub-dossier concluded on 23 February 2026 with no prosecution recommended against Moreau, Pierre Meyers, or a further board member. No trial has occurred and no verdict has been reached in any Nethysgate sub-dossier as of this writing; accordingly, beneficiaries and board members beyond Moreau's confirmed dismissal are described here by role only, not named as convicted.

Sources: L'Avenir, "Le parquet veut renvoyer 10 suspects," 11 December 2025 · RTBF, "Stéphane Moreau sera licencié," October 2019 · DH, government annuls bonus decision, 13 December 2019 · La Libre, on the disputed "golden parachute" amount

2.6 Kazakhgate (2011 law change; investigation closed 2019 without any conviction)

Institution: The Belgian Parliament (which amended the Code of Criminal Procedure) and the Brussels prosecutor's office. Mechanism: In 2011, Belgium broadened its "extended criminal settlement" procedure (transaction pénale élargie), allowing defendants — even after indictment, even for money-laundering and fraud charges — to extinguish criminal prosecution by paying a settlement. Within days of the law's passage, it was applied retroactively to three Kazakh-Uzbek businessmen (Patokh Chodiev, Alijan Ibragimov, and Alexander Machkevitch, associated with the ENRC mining group) then under investigation in Belgium for money laundering and forgery. On 17 June 2011, Brussels prosecutors dropped the case after the trio paid a settlement reported at roughly €21–23 million in total. Belgian and French reporting alleged that French government figures lobbied Belgium on the trio's behalf, with contacts running through the trio's French lawyer and former Belgian Senate President Armand De Decker, who received €734,000 for consulting work in the period — a sum investigators viewed as disproportionate to the work performed and potentially indicative of influence peddling.

Legal outcome: - Armand De Decker was formally indicted for influence peddling in May 2018 over his 2011 activities. He was never tried: he died on 12 June 2019, and Belgian prosecutors confirmed that the criminal action against him was thereby extinguished by his death — not resolved by acquittal or dismissal on the merits. Because he was never convicted and did not resign from any office over this matter (he was a private lawyer/former senator by that point), he is not named in this dossier's People Index as convicted or resigned — the case against him simply ended with his death. La Libre, 13 June 2019 · RTBF, on his 2018 indictment - Didier Reynders, then Belgian Finance Minister and later an EU Commissioner, was never charged or indicted in connection with Kazakhgate. He has consistently denied any involvement in the 2011 law change. A parliamentary inquiry commission's president publicly voiced suspicion of Reynders and his political network, but this was a political, not judicial, accusation. A separate corruption allegation against him from a former state security officer was closed without further action in 2016 for lack of evidence. Reynders does not meet this dossier's inclusion bar and is not named as implicated in any wrongdoing. BX1, on the 2016 dismissal of the separate allegation · RTBF, on the parliamentary commission's accusations

Editorial note: Kazakhgate is included here for context on Belgium's legal framework (the 2011 settlement law and its later reform) but produces no named "convicted" or "resigned" individuals under this dossier's inclusion rule — the case is presented to illustrate how a scandal can leave the legal record without a legal resolution, rather than as an example of an adjudicated corruption case.

Sources: Wikipedia — Kazakhgate · La Libre, 13 June 2019 · RTBF, 2018 indictment · BX1, 2016 dismissal of separate Reynders allegation
