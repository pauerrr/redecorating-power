Compiled for a Domestic Data Streamers workshop. Sources are cited inline. Individuals are named only where they meet a strict legal-inclusion rule (see note below). This is a research compilation for educational/journalistic purposes, comparable to Transparency International or OCCRP country profiles.

Inclusion rule applied throughout: a person is named as "convicted" only if they hold a final ("prawomocny") criminal conviction for a corruption-related offense, or is named as "resigned" only if they resigned from office over an extensively documented scandal — labelled explicitly as "resigned, not convicted." People who are only accused, indicted, under active/ongoing trial, or whose case has been dismissed short of conviction are described by role/institution only, with the legal status stated explicitly.

1. Country snapshot

  • Transparency International Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI) 2024 (published 11 Feb 2025): Poland scored 53/100, ranking 52nd of 180 countries, essentially flat versus the prior year. Transparency.org — Poland 2024
  • CPI 2023: Poland scored 54/100. Transparency International's 2023 press release noted "Poland's seven-point decline over the last decade underscores the systematic efforts by the previous ruling Law and Justice (PiS) party to monopolise power at the expense of public interest." Transparency.org — Poland 2023 | TI press release, 2023 CPI
  • Poland's CPI score peaked at 62 in 2015 and fell to the low-to-mid 50s by 2023–2024, a decline Transparency International attributes largely to the erosion of judicial independence during PiS's 2015–2023 governments.
  • Eurobarometer / Global Corruption Barometer: the EU-wide 2024 Special Eurobarometer on corruption found 68% of EU citizens consider corruption widespread in their country; a Poland-specific figure for that exact survey wave could not be independently confirmed for this dossier. A precisely sourced and comparable figure is Transparency International's Global Corruption Barometer (fieldwork Oct–Dec 2020, published 2021): 72% of Poles said corruption is a major problem in their country (vs. 62% EU average), 37% thought it had worsened in the previous year, and only 23% believed ordinary citizens can make a difference against corruption — the worst result of any country surveyed, with 57% saying they cannot. European Commission Eurobarometer 2024 | Batory Foundation analysis of the Global Corruption Barometer, republished by Notes from Poland
  • EU funds frozen — rule-of-law conditionality: the European Commission opened an Article 7(1) TEU procedure against Poland in December 2017 over judicial reforms threatening judicial independence; it was formally withdrawn in May 2024 after the change of government. Under three separate EU instruments (cohesion/development funds, the National Recovery and Resilience Plan/KPO, and the 2020 Rule of Law Conditionality Regulation), the Commission had suspended a combined total of roughly €137 billion — approximately €76.5 billion in cohesion funds and €59.8 billion in KPO recovery funds — around 17% of Polish GDP at the time. Centre for European Reform, "Freezing EU Funds" | Euronews breakdown
  • On 29 February 2024, the European Commission approved unblocking access to up to €137 billion in EU funds for Poland, citing judicial and rule-of-law reforms under the Tusk government. The first actual disbursement of €6.3 billion under the KPO recovery plan (€3.6bn loans, €2.7bn grants) followed on 15 April 2024. European Commission Inforegio, 29 Feb 2024 | Euronews, 15 Apr 2024
  • GRECO (Council of Europe): Poland's Fourth Evaluation Round found only 8 of 22 anti-corruption recommendations fully implemented (9 partial, 5 not implemented) — rated "globally unsatisfactory." Its Fifth Evaluation Round found only 3 of 21 recommendations satisfactorily implemented (8 partial, 10 not implemented). Poland was placed under non-compliance procedures for both rounds (November 2023 and December 2024 respectively), though GRECO acknowledged the Tusk government's efforts to restore independence of the National Council of the Judiciary, requesting a further progress report by 30 November 2025. Council of Europe, GRECO Fourth Round report on Poland | eucrim, GRECO Fifth Round summary on Poland

2. Major cases and systems

2.1 Rywingate — the Lew Rywin affair (2002–2006)

Institutions: Gazeta Wyborcza / Agora S.A.; the Sejm (lower house); the government of PM Leszek Miller (SLD).

The scandal: On 22 July 2002, film producer Lew Rywin approached Adam Michnik, editor-in-chief of Gazeta Wyborcza, and solicited a $17.5 million bribe in exchange for arranging favorable amendments to draft media legislation that would have allowed Agora S.A. (the paper's publisher) to acquire a national television station. Rywin claimed to represent an anonymous "group in power." Michnik secretly recorded the conversation and later made it public, triggering a parliamentary investigation. Wikipedia, Rywin affair

Legal outcome — final conviction:

  • 26 April 2004: a Warsaw court found Rywin guilty of attempted fraud, sentencing him to 2 years and 6 months' imprisonment plus a 100,000 złoty fine. Both Rywin and the prosecution appealed.
  • 10 December 2004: the Warsaw Court of Appeals re-classified the conviction as "paid protection" (płatna protekcja) — influence-peddling on behalf of an anonymous group — resetting the sentence to 2 years' imprisonment and retaining the fine. The Supreme Court subsequently dismissed the parties' cassation appeals, making the conviction final.
  • Rywin began serving his sentence, was released on health grounds on 31 May 2005 pending medical assessment, recalled to prison in October 2005, and granted parole in October 2006 with a two-year probation period. Wikipedia, Rywin affair | Alamy/AP photo caption, 31 May 2005

Legal status: convicted, final (2004–2005).

Web of connections, and who was excluded: a Sejm investigatory committee's minority report (authored by PiS deputy Zbigniew Ziobro, adopted by the Sejm in September 2004) named then-Prime Minister Leszek Miller and several other SLD officials as alleged masterminds behind Rywin's approach. None of these officials were ever criminally charged or convicted — only Rywin faced and received a criminal conviction. Per the strict inclusion rule, Miller and the other named SLD officials are excluded from this dossier's "convicted" category. Wikipedia, Rywin affair

Aftermath: Rywingate became shorthand in Polish politics for high-level media/political collusion and contributed to the SLD's collapse in the 2005 election.

2.2 Mariusz Kamiński and Maciej Wąsik — the CBA "land scandal" and the double-pardon affair (2007–2024)

Institutions: Centralne Biuro Antykorupcyjne (CBA, Central Anti-Corruption Bureau); the Presidency of the Republic of Poland; the Sejm; the Supreme Court; the Constitutional Tribunal.

The case: Under the first PiS government (2005–2007), Mariusz Kamiński headed the newly created CBA, with Maciej Wąsik as his deputy. In 2007, CBA officers ran a sting/entrapment operation targeting officials in an alleged land-privatization corruption scheme (the "land scandal," afera gruntowa) — an operation that controversially precipitated the resignation of deputy PM Andrzej Lepper and contributed to the collapse of the PiS government that year. Notes from Poland, 20 Dec 2023

Timeline to finality:

  • 2009: Kamiński and three subordinates, including Wąsik, were charged with exceeding their powers ("abuse of power") in conducting the sting operation.
  • 2015 (first-instance conviction): a Warsaw court found the four CBA officials guilty; Kamiński was sentenced to 3 years' imprisonment plus a 10-year ban on holding public office, with Wąsik and two others receiving comparable sentences. All four appealed.
  • November 2015 (first presidential pardon): President Andrzej Duda pardoned all four — issued one day after Kamiński was appointed a minister coordinating Poland's security services, and before their appeals had been heard, i.e. before the conviction was final. Legal experts widely argued the pardon was invalid, since Polish law reserves presidential pardons for final ("prawomocne") convictions.
  • 3 June 2023: Poland's PiS-aligned Constitutional Tribunal ruled the Supreme Court had no authority to assess the pardon's validity, upholding Duda's position.
  • 6 June 2023: Poland's Supreme Court nonetheless declared the 2015 pardon invalid, as it had been issued before the conviction was final — reopening the path to a full appeal.
  • 20 December 2023 (final conviction): the Warsaw Regional Court (Sąd Okręgowy w Warszawie) upheld the abuse-of-power convictions on appeal, reducing the sentences to 2 years' imprisonment and the public-office ban to 5 years. The ruling was explicitly reported as "prawomocne" — legally final and binding. Notes from Poland, 20 Dec 2023
  • 9 January 2024: Kamiński and Wąsik, having refused to report for imprisonment, took refuge inside the Presidential Palace; police entered and arrested them the same day. Notes from Poland, 9 Jan 2024 | CNN, 10 Jan 2024
  • 23–24 January 2024 (second presidential pardon): President Duda issued a fresh pardon, this time targeting individuals holding a final conviction (correcting the earlier legal defect); both men were released from prison. Euronews, 24 Jan 2024

Legal status: convicted, final (20 December 2023, Warsaw Regional Court) — 2 years' imprisonment and a 5-year public-office ban — subsequently pardoned by presidential act after roughly two weeks' imprisonment. This is a rare case where a final criminal conviction and a presidential pardon coexist: the pardon does not erase the historical fact of the final conviction, but the two men are not serving the sentence and the office-holding ban was controversially lifted.

Aftermath: the affair deepened a constitutional standoff between the Duda presidency and the Tusk government over the independence of Poland's courts and the limits of the pardon power.

2.3 The Health Ministry mask and ventilator procurement scandal — resignations (2020)

Institution: Ministry of Health.

The mask deal: in March 2020, at the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, Poland's Ministry of Health purchased masks worth around 5 million złoty through a ski instructor personally connected to Health Minister Łukasz Szumowski's family, a deal facilitated via Szumowski's brother and Deputy Health Minister Janusz Cieszyński. Poland's Central Institute for Labour Protection found the masks did not meet required sanitary standards. Notes from Poland, 15 May 2020

The ventilator deal: the Ministry separately awarded a €44.5 million contract to businessman Andrzej Izdebski — a former arms dealer with no relevant medical-equipment experience — to supply 1,241 ventilators. Only around 200 were delivered, found defective, and never used in a hospital, despite an advance payment of roughly €35 million having already been made. Opposition MP Michał Szczerba called it "a gigantic scandal." Izdebski died in Albania in 2022; Albanian prosecutors ruled the death was from natural causes. Balkan Insight | Dawn.com

Legal outcome: Deputy Health Minister Janusz Cieszyński, who signed the ventilator contract, resigned in August 2020; Health Minister Łukasz Szumowski resigned on 18 August 2020, one day after his deputy, amid the mask and ventilator procurement controversies. Forbes, 18 Aug 2020 | Notes from Poland, 19 Aug 2020

Legal status: resigned, not convicted (both officials, August 2020). No final criminal conviction was identified for Szumowski, Cieszyński, or Izdebski in connection with these procurements.

2.4 Context note: Sławomir Nowak — case ongoing, no final conviction

Former Polish Minister of Transport (2011–2013) and later head of Ukraine's state road agency Ukravtodor, Sławomir Nowak was arrested by Poland's Central Anti-Corruption Bureau on 20 July 2020, in a joint investigation with Ukraine's NABU, on suspicion of corruption, directing an organized criminal group, and money laundering, allegedly involving more than 6.1 million złoty in bribes tied to Ukrainian road-repair contracts. Wikipedia, Sławomir Nowak | UNIAN, 20 Jul 2020

A narrower "Polish thread" of bribery allegations against Nowak was dismissed by a Polish court before trial for lack of factual basis — a final dismissal, but not a conviction. The core "Ukrainian thread" of the case went to trial before the Warsaw Regional Court starting July 2024 and remained ongoing as of the most recent available reporting, with procedural disputes over whether part of the case involving Ukrainian co-defendants should be transferred to Ukrainian courts. rp.pl | PAP

Per the strict inclusion rule, Nowak is excluded from this dossier's "convicted" category: no final conviction exists, and the core case remains an active, unresolved trial.

2.5 Context note: GetBack S.A. financial scandal — no final criminal convictions

GetBack S.A., a Polish debt-collection company, collapsed in spring 2018 after revelations it had issued fraudulent bonds for years, with losses to more than 9,000 bondholders estimated at PLN 2.5–3.5 billion (roughly €600–800 million). Former CEO Konrad Kąkolewski was arrested in June 2018 and held in pre-trial detention until May 2022. Poland's Financial Supervision Authority (KNF) separately imposed an administrative fine exceeding 3 million złoty on Kąkolewski for disclosure violations — an administrative penalty, not a criminal conviction. A criminal trial involving 16 defendants, with an indictment running to 1,200 pages and case files spanning 1,600 volumes, remained ongoing before the Warsaw District Court as of the most recent reporting, with fewer than a third of defendants having completed questioning. getback.wiki | money.pl

Per the strict inclusion rule, GetBack is excluded from this dossier's "convicted" category: the criminal trial remains ongoing and no final conviction has been secured against any defendant.

2.6 Context note: the Justice Fund (Fundusz Sprawiedliwości) affair — indictment and fugitive status, no trial held

The Justice Fund, created to assist crime victims, is alleged to have been converted into a political slush fund during Zbigniew Ziobro's tenure as Justice Minister (2015–2023). Poland's National Prosecutor's Office opened a formal investigation in February 2024; prosecutors allege Ziobro committed 26 offenses, including founding and leading an organized criminal group within the ministry that diverted more than 150 million złoty from the fund toward political allies and the purchase of Pegasus spyware used to surveil opposition figures, prosecutors, and journalists. The Sejm stripped Ziobro of immunity in October 2024. Ziobro left Poland in late 2024, was granted political asylum in Hungary, later left Hungary, and was reported in the United States as of the most recent available reporting, with Poland's Parliament having made an arrest warrant legally binding in November 2025, opening a path toward a formal extradition request. rp.pl | Brussels Signal, 20 May 2026

Per the strict inclusion rule, Ziobro is excluded from this dossier's "convicted" category: he is indicted on 26 counts but has not stood trial, remains a fugitive, and holds no final conviction or resignation-over-scandal that meets the inclusion bar (he left office at the natural end of the PiS government's term in 2023, rather than resigning specifically over this scandal).

6. Methodology and workshop-use notes

On sourcing: every factual claim above carries an inline citation, drawn primarily from Notes from Poland, Transparency International, the European Commission, the Centre for European Reform, and Polish national press (rp.pl, money.pl, PAP) reporting on court proceedings. Where a precise figure could not be independently confirmed for this dossier — such as the Poland-specific percentage in the most recent Special Eurobarometer wave — this is flagged explicitly and a comparable, precisely sourced alternative figure (the Global Corruption Barometer) is used instead rather than guessed at.

On the pardon-and-conviction paradox: the Kamiński/Wąsik case (2.2) is presented in full chronological detail because its legal status is genuinely unusual: a final criminal conviction (20 December 2023) coexists with a presidential pardon (January 2024) issued after the men had already begun serving their sentence. This dossier treats the historical fact of the final conviction as reportable in its own right, while also reporting the pardon and its effects, rather than treating the pardon as erasing the conviction's having occurred. Facilitators should note this is a deliberately precise legal framing, not an implication that the men are currently imprisoned or barred from office — they are neither, as a result of the pardon.

On excluded but newsworthy material: three of the most widely discussed alleged corruption stories in recent Polish politics — the Sławomir Nowak case, the GetBack S.A. collapse, and the Justice Fund/Ziobro affair — are deliberately excluded from this dossier's "convicted" category because none has produced a final criminal conviction as of the most recent available reporting. This is not an editorial judgment about guilt or innocence; it reflects strict adherence to the inclusion rule requiring legal finality. Facilitators wishing to discuss these cases in a workshop setting should present them explicitly as "ongoing prosecutions" or "unresolved allegations," not as established corruption.

On EU conditionality as a Poland-specific case study: unlike Hungary, where EU funds remain frozen as of mid-2025, Poland's experience (Section 1) shows the conditionality mechanism operating as designed: funds were suspended over documented judicial-independence concerns, and were substantially released within months of a change of government implementing the reforms the Commission had asked for. This makes Poland a useful contrast case for workshop discussions about whether EU financial pressure can produce rule-of-law change.
