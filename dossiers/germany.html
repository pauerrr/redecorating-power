1. Country Snapshot

  • Transparency International CPI 2024: Germany scored 75/100, ranked 15th of 180 — its lowest-ever score on the index. Source: Transparency.org CPI 2024
  • CPI 2025: Germany scored 77/100, ranked 10th of 182 — up 2 points and 5 places versus 2024, though still down 4 points over the last decade. Sources: Transparency.org CPI 2025, GSK Stockmann
  • Eurobarometer: EU-wide, 68% of citizens believe corruption is widespread in their country (2024 survey). In Germany specifically, 57% consider corruption widespread nationally — for comparison, Greece 98%, Finland 18%. Sources: Eurobarometer 3217, Euronews
  • Cost-of-corruption estimates (figures vary substantially by methodology — cite as a range, not a single number):
  • Cum-Ex: minimum estimated damage to the German treasury of ≥€10 billion, concentrated 2001–2011. — Finanzwende
  • Cum-Cum: independent estimate of €28.5 billion German-specific damage (2000–2020) versus the German government's own more conservative estimate of €7.5 billion. — Handelsblatt
  • Combined Cum-Ex/Cum-Cum-style trading, per a 12-country Correctiv investigation: ≥€150 billion total European damage, of which ~€36 billion was lost specifically to German tax authorities. — LTO
  • GRECO: The Fifth Round Evaluation found Germany in only partial/insufficient compliance with recommendations on preventing corruption in central government and law enforcement; GRECO requested a further progress report by 31 March 2026 (concluded August 2025). Separately, the Fourth Round evaluation (parliamentarians, judges, prosecutors) found Germany had one of the highest non-implementation rates for recommendations concerning MPs: 16.6% (versus 10.9% for judges, 8.4% for prosecutors). Sources: Council of Europe, eucrim
  • Official crime statistics (BKA Bundeslagebild Korruption): 3,841 registered corruption offenses in 2023 (+6.7% YoY); 2,926 in 2024 (−23.8% YoY), with recorded financial damage falling to €36 million (−36.8% YoY). Sources: BKA 2023, BKA 2024

2. Major Cases

Case 1 — CDU Illegal Party Donations Scandal (CDU-Spendenaffäre), 1993–2000

Institution: CDU federal party, under the chairmanship of Helmut Kohl.

Mechanism: Off-the-books "black accounts" held undeclared cash donations — including from arms dealer Karlheinz Schreiber — via illegal foreign bank accounts in Switzerland and Liechtenstein, concealing more than 30 million Deutschmarks, in violation of Germany's Party Finance Act.

Legal status — precise: - Helmut Kohl (former Chancellor, CDU Chairman): was NOT criminally convicted. The investigation for breach of trust (Untreue) against him was discontinued by the Bonn Regional Court (Landgericht Bonn) in February 2001 under §153a StPO, against payment of a DM 300,000 money penalty (Geldauflage), citing minor culpability. This is a procedural settlement, not a verdict of guilt, and must never be described as a "conviction." Kohl maintained he had given his "word of honor" (Ehrenwort) not to name four or five donors — a justification later disputed by fellow CDU figure Wolfgang Schäuble. Sources: Washington Post, CNN, Tagesspiegel - Walther Leisler Kiep (former CDU Federal Treasurer): Convicted of tax evasion by the Landgericht Augsburg in 2001, for failing to declare roughly 16,000 DM in interest income on his 1991 tax return. Fined DM 45,000. Sources: Tagesspiegel, Wikipedia

Aftermath: A Bundestag investigative committee was established in December 1999; the scandal triggered reform of German party-finance law and lasting reputational damage to the CDU, even though its chairman was never criminally convicted.

Sources: CDU-Spendenaffäre, Wikipedia DE, Bundestag archive

Case 2 — Cum-Ex Trading Scandal, c. 2001–2012 (prosecutions continuing into 2026)

Institution: Private banks, law firms, and investors exploiting a dividend-tax loophole.

Mechanism: Coordinated share trades around dividend record dates among multiple parties generated duplicate tax-refund certificates, allowing a tax paid once to be reclaimed multiple times.

Legal status — Hanno Berger (lawyer, designer of the scheme): FINAL, doubly convicted. - Landgericht Bonn, December 2022: convicted of especially serious tax evasion in three cases, sentenced to 8 years, plus repayment of €13.6 million. The Bundesgerichtshof (BGH) rejected his appeal in September 2023, making this conviction final. - Landgericht Wiesbaden, 30 May 2023: separately convicted of especially serious tax evasion in three further cases (trades from 2006–2008 involving the late Berlin entrepreneur Rafael Roth, with €113 million improperly reclaimed), sentenced to 8 years 3 months. The BGH rejected this appeal on 29 October 2024, making it final; a further BGH ruling on 8 July 2025 confirmed confiscation of €1.1 million in criminal proceeds. - May 2026: the Landgericht Bonn consolidated both final sentences into a combined total sentence (Gesamtstrafe) of 10 years. - Berger was extradited from Switzerland to Germany in 2022 to face these charges and continues to deny wrongdoing.

Scale: German investigators have, per reporting, pursued roughly 1,500 suspects tied to Cum-Ex overall; most individual cases beyond Berger's remain unresolved or in progress and are not named here as convictions.

Sources: LTO — Bonn verdict, LTO — BGH confirms Bonn, LTO — BGH confirms Wiesbaden, Hessenschau, beck-aktuell — consolidated sentence

Case 3 — Maskenaffäre (COVID-19 mask procurement scandal), 2020–2021

Institution: CDU/CSU members of the Bundestag and Bavarian Landtag acting as paid intermediaries in pandemic-era PPE procurement.

Legal status — precise, per person: - Georg Nüsslein (CSU, then-Bundestag member) and Alfred Sauter (CSU, Bavarian state MP, former Bavarian Justice Minister): both brokered mask-supply deals for commissions (Nüsslein reportedly earned €660,000 on a roughly €35 million deal). Neither was criminally convicted. The Bundesgerichtshof ruled in July 2022 that brokering the deals for commission did not legally constitute punishable bribery of a mandate-holder under German law, because the favor did not concern the "exercise of the mandate" in the narrow legal sense. Nüsslein's seized assets (€3.6 million) were ordered released to him. Both must be named only by role/accusation, never as "convicted." Sources: Berliner Zeitung, Transparency Deutschland - Nikolas Löbel (CDU, Bundestag member, Mannheim): brokered a separate PPE delivery contract and received a €250,000 commission. He resigned his Bundestag mandate and district chairmanship in March 2021, publicly apologizing: "Dafür möchte ich mich bei allen Bürgerinnen und Bürgern dieses Landes entschuldigen." Prosecutors in Stuttgart announced at the end of March 2021 that they would not open a formal investigation for lack of initial suspicion — so there was no criminal case at all, only the resignation. This is a clean, sourced example of "resignation over documented scandal." Sources: Wikipedia, t-online - Andrea Tandler (businesswoman, daughter of former CSU official Gerold Tandler — a private individual, not herself a politician): earned an estimated €48 million in commissions from mask deals. Convicted of tax evasion by the Landgericht München I in December 2023, originally sentenced to 4 years 5 months. On appeal, the Bundesgerichtshof reduced the sentence on 20 April 2025 (case 1 StR 238/24), partially discontinuing one charge for insufficient evidence, to 3 years — now final/rechtskräftig. Her partner was correspondingly convicted with a final reduced sentence of 3 years. Sources: LTO — original verdict, LTO — BGH reduction, Tagesspiegel

Aftermath: A Bavarian Landtag investigative committee ("Untersuchungsausschuss Maske") ran from December 2021 to May 2023 and exposed a legal gap — facilitation-for-profit outside formal parliamentary duties was not criminally punishable — prompting Transparency Deutschland to call for criminal-law reform.

Sources: abgeordnetenwatch, Bayerischer Landtag

Case 4 — Wirecard Collapse, 2015–2020 (criminal trial not yet concluded as of mid-2026)

Institution: Wirecard AG, and the supervisory failures of financial regulator BaFin and auditor EY.

Mechanism: Fabrication of approximately €1.9 billion in fictitious cash balances (supposedly held in Philippine trustee accounts) and fraudulent Asian revenue, inflating the balance sheet until the company's June 2020 collapse — Germany's largest post-war corporate fraud.

Legal status — precise: no individual final criminal conviction can be reported. - Markus Braun (former CEO): his criminal trial at the Landgericht München I (begun December 2022, the longest economic-crime trial in German history) remains ongoing and unconcluded as of this dossier's compilation in mid-2026, with a verdict anticipated in 2026 but no confirmed date. Prosecutors have signaled they will seek a sentence of "more than ten years" if he is convicted. Braun must not be described as convicted — he denies all charges and blames the fugitive former COO and a confessed co-defendant. A separate civil judgment (not a criminal conviction) ordered Braun and other former executives to pay €140 million in civil damages — this is a distinct legal track and must not be conflated with a criminal conviction. - Oliver Bellenhaus (former Wirecard Dubai representative, confessed and cooperating): released from over three years of pre-trial detention under strict conditions; has confessed to fabricating contracts, but no final verdict/sentence for him was confirmed as of mid-2026 — his case remains part of the same unconcluded Munich trial track. - Jan Marsalek (former COO): fled Germany on 18 June 2020 and remains a fugitive with an active international arrest warrant, reportedly living in Moscow under a Russian identity and alleged by investigators to be a Russian intelligence asset since around 2010. He has not been tried.

Institutional findings (no personal guilt asserted): The Bundestag's Third Committee of Inquiry (19th electoral term, active November 2020 – June 2021; 32 sessions, 110 witnesses) concluded that the auditor EY and the regulator BaFin both failed professionally — BaFin acted too slowly, initially treating Wirecard as a "victim of speculators" and imposing a short-selling ban on its shares rather than investigating the company; EY focused disproportionately on the regulated banking subsidiary and gave insufficient scrutiny to Wirecard AG itself, where the fraud occurred. The 2021 "Act to Strengthen Financial Market Integrity" tightened balance-sheet controls, audit rules, and BaFin's investigative powers as a direct legislative consequence.

Sources: LTO, Handelsblatt, LTO on Bellenhaus release, Wikipedia — Marsalek, Bundestag final report, bpb.de

Case 5 — Philipp Amthor / Augustus Intelligence Lobbying Affair, 2020 (reputational, non-criminal)

Precise account: CDU Bundestag member Philipp Amthor lobbied the Federal Economics Ministry on behalf of the U.S. AI start-up Augustus Intelligence, arranging ministry meetings for the company. Months later he received 2,817 stock options and a director position from the company. He publicly denied wrongdoing at the time ("Ich bin nicht käuflich" — "I am not for sale"). In August 2020 he ended the arrangement and returned the stock options and directorship. He did not resign his Bundestag seat; he did withdraw his candidacy for CDU state chairman in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern. The episode is properly characterized as a reputational lobbying scandal with a partial rollback of outside roles — not a resignation from elected office, and not a criminal case.

Sources: abgeordnetenwatch, Tagesspiegel

Case 6 — Christian Wulff / "Kreditaffäre" (resignation, later acquitted)

Precise account: In December 2011, allegations surfaced that Christian Wulff, then Federal President (previously Minister-President of Lower Saxony), had inaccurately answered a state-parliament inquiry about his home financing, concealing a favorable private loan, and separately faced allegations of accepting hospitality from a film producer. On 16 February 2012, Hanover prosecutors announced sufficient indications of an initial suspicion of accepting advantages (Vorteilsannahme) and requested the lifting of his presidential immunity. Wulff resigned as Federal President on 17 February 2012, citing the loss of trust required for the office — a legitimate, well-documented example of resignation over scandal, occurring before any trial. In February 2014, the Landgericht Hannover acquitted Wulff of the charge, a final/rechtskräftig acquittal. He must therefore be excluded from any "convicted" list — only the resignation itself is reportable as fact.

Sources: Wulff-Affäre, Wikipedia DE, LTO, Bundespräsident.de — resignation speech

Case 7 — Bundeswehr "Berateraffäre" (external consultants scandal), 2018–2020 (institutional, not a corruption conviction)

Institution: Federal Ministry of Defence, under then-Defence Minister Ursula von der Leyen.

Mechanism: External IT and management consultants were awarded contracts worth a low three-digit-million-euro sum for defense modernization projects, with the Bundestag's investigative committee documenting numerous procedural and legal violations in how those contracts were awarded (including inadequate documentation, potential circumvention of public-procurement rules, and close personal ties between ministry staff and consulting firms).

Legal status: The Bundestag investigative committee's final report made no legal or direct political accusation of personal wrongdoing against von der Leyen. Berlin prosecutors separately requested Bundestag files to assess whether to open criminal investigations, including into deleted data on her official mobile phones, but no criminal charges or convictions against her or named subordinates were confirmed. This case is presented here as an institutional mismanagement and oversight scandal — illustrating systemic procurement weaknesses — not as a corruption conviction. Von der Leyen left the Defence Ministry in 2019 to become European Commission President, a transition unrelated in timing to any resignation-over-scandal framing, and she must not be listed as convicted or as having resigned over this affair.

Aftermath: The episode prompted tightened Bundestag oversight of ministry procurement processes and stricter documentation requirements for external consulting contracts across federal ministries.

Sources: bundeswehr-journal, t-online

Notable exclusions (per the strict legal rule)

  • Georg Nüsslein and Alfred Sauter — BGH found no punishable offense in July 2022; never convicted.
  • Markus Braun and Oliver Bellenhaus — Wirecard criminal trial unconcluded as of mid-2026; not convicted.
  • Jan Marsalek — fugitive, untried.
  • Christian Wulff and David Groenewold — Wulff acquitted (final, 2014); Groenewold acquitted of the main charge, formally warned only for a false affidavit in a related matter.
  • Philipp Amthor — no criminal case; did not resign his Bundestag seat.

Christian Wulff, resignation statement, 17 February 2012: said Germany needs a president backed by "the trust not only of a majority, but of a broad majority of citizens," and that recent developments had substantially impaired that trust — resigning while denying the more serious allegations, of which he was later acquitted. — Bundespräsident.de
