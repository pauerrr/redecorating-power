Compiled for a Domestic Data Streamers workshop repository. Research date: July 2, 2026.

Legal methodology (strictly applied throughout): a named individual appears in this dossier only if (a) they have received a FINAL criminal conviction for a corruption-related offense — meaning the sentence has been upheld on appeal and is no longer under appeal ("transitado em julgado"), or (b) they resigned from public office over an extensively documented scandal, with an exact date and stated reason. People who are merely accused ("arguidos"), on trial, acquitted, or whose appeals are still pending are explicitly excluded from the "convicted" framing, even where their cases are otherwise newsworthy. Where such people must be mentioned for context (because they are part of the web of a qualifying case), they are referred to only by role, not treated as convicted.

1. Country Snapshot

  • Transparency International Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI):
  • 2024: score 57/100, rank 43rd of 180 countries — described by Transparency International as Portugal's worst result to date, a fall of 9 places versus 2023. Source: transparency.org/en/cpi/2024/index/prt
  • 2025: score 56/100, rank 46th of 182 countries (published 10 February 2026). Source: transparency.org/en/cpi/2025/index/prt

  • Portugal sits below the Western Europe/EU average but ahead of Spain, Italy, Poland, and Greece. The global average CPI score fell to a record low of 42 in 2025. Source: Portugal Decoded, CPI 2025 analysis

  • Eurobarometer corruption perception (Special Eurobarometer on Corruption, published July 2025):

  • 91% of Portuguese respondents consider corruption widespread in Portugal (EU average: 69%) — the 3rd highest figure in the EU, behind Greece (97%) and Croatia (92%).
  • 64% feel personally affected by corruption in daily life (EU average: 30%).
  • 75% of Portuguese companies consider corruption widespread (EU average: 63%); 50% consider it a problem when doing business (EU average: 35%).
  • 98% say giving money to public administration for a favor is "never acceptable" — the highest such rate in the EU.

  • Sources: Euronews, 23 July 2025; European Commission Eurobarometer survey detail; EU 2025 Rule of Law Report — Portugal chapter

  • Estimated cost of corruption: approximately €18.2 billion per year, roughly 7.9% of GDP, per a Greens/EFA (European Parliament group) report "The Costs of Corruption Across the EU." This figure exceeds Portugal's annual health budget (€16.2bn) and is roughly ten times annual unemployment-benefit spending (€1.8bn); per-capita equivalent is about €1,763/year. Sources: Algarve Daily News; Greens/EFA full report (PDF)

  • GRECO (Group of States against Corruption, Council of Europe) compliance:

  • Fifth Evaluation Round (top executive functions and law enforcement), Compliance Report GrecoRC5(2025)7, adopted 17–19 March 2025, published 2 September 2025: of 28 recommendations, 0 are fully implemented, 18 partly implemented, 10 not implemented. GRECO's own conclusion: "Portugal has satisfactorily implemented none of the 28 recommendations." Key concern: neither the GNR nor PSP (police forces) has a dedicated anti-corruption strategy; disciplinary proceedings remain almost entirely in the hands of officers' own hierarchical superiors with no independent oversight; the police watchdog IGAI is understaffed (38 of 55 planned posts filled, down from 42 in January 2024). Next progress report due 30 September 2026. Source: GRECO Compliance Report, Council of Europe (PDF)
  • Fourth Evaluation Round (parliamentarians, judges, prosecutors): Portugal has satisfactorily implemented only 5 of 15 recommendations, rated "globally unsatisfactory," with a further report requested by 31 March 2026. Lobbying remains unregulated; no conflict-of-interest impact assessment has been carried out for MPs. Sources: Council of Europe/GRECO statement; eucrim.eu summary

2. Major Cases

2.1 Operação Marquês (José Sócrates) — STILL ON TRIAL, NO FINAL CONVICTION

Years active: Investigation opened 2009–2014; Sócrates detained 21 November 2014; trial formally opened 3 July 2025; ongoing as of July 2026. Institution involved: Office of the Prime Minister (2005–2011); state-linked infrastructure and banking sectors. Mechanism: Prosecutors allege a scheme of political corruption, money laundering, and forgery connected to public contracts and financial favors during and after Sócrates's premiership, run through offshore structures and front companies.

Legal status — precisely stated: - Sócrates was formally indicted in 2017 on 31 counts (3 passive corruption, 16 money laundering, 9 forgery, 3 qualified tax fraud). - On 9 April 2021, instructing judge Ivo Rosa dismissed the corruption and tax-fraud charges as unsubstantiated ("based on speculation and fantasy"), sending him to trial on only 6 counts (money laundering and forgery). Euronews, 10 April 2021 - On 25 January 2024, the Lisbon Court of Appeal (Tribunal da Relação de Lisboa) partially reversed that decision, reinstating charges: Sócrates was ordered to trial on 3 corruption counts, 13 money-laundering counts, and 6 tax-fraud counts, alongside 21 co-defendants (118 crimes total). Observador, 25 January 2024; Público, 25 January 2024 - Sócrates's subsequent appeals against reinstatement were rejected by the Constitutional Court (10 January 2025) and the Supreme Court (February 2026). - The criminal trial opened 3 July 2025 at Lisbon's Campus de Justiça with 21 defendants. SOL, 3 July 2025 - Through late 2025 and into 2026 the trial has been repeatedly suspended by a cascade of defense-lawyer resignations and representation disputes; prosecutors have warned that some charges risk expiring by prescription. Document-forgery charges against Sócrates have already been formally extinguished by prescription; separately, corruption charges tied to one strand of the case ("Vale do Lobo") were reported at risk of prescribing in the first half of 2026. The Portugal Post, 25 February 2026; Público, 11 November 2025 - As of 2 July 2026, José Sócrates has no final conviction of any kind — not for corruption, money laundering, tax fraud, or forgery. The only judicial ruling to conclude in this matter is a 29–30 June 2026 decision by the Lisbon Administrative Court ordering the Portuguese State (not Sócrates) to pay him €15,000 in damages for a breach of judicial secrecy during the original investigation — a civil ruling against the state with no bearing on his guilt or innocence in the pending criminal trial, which the Ministério Público is appealing. ECO, 29 June 2026; Observador, 30 June 2026

On the 2011 resignation: Sócrates resigned as Prime Minister on 23 March 2011, immediately after Parliament rejected his government's fourth austerity package — a no-confidence/debt-crisis event. Portugal's EU/IMF bailout request followed on 6 April 2011. This resignation was not framed at the time as related to corruption; the Operação Marquês investigation and his arrest came more than three years later, in November 2014, and are treated by every contemporary source as a separate matter. Sources: Deseret News, 23 March 2011; Forbes, 23 March 2011

Because Sócrates does not meet either criterion of this dossier's legal rule (no final conviction; his 2011 resignation was not a corruption resignation), he is included here only as a description of an unresolved, extensively reported case — not as a "convicted" or "resigned-over-corruption" individual. No sentence or conviction is attributed to him in this document.

Sources: Euronews Portugal, 17 March 2025 trial preview; Renascença explainer, 22 March 2024; Público, 25 January 2024

2.2 BES / Grupo Espírito Santo Collapse (Ricardo Salgado) — TWO FINAL CONVICTIONS; SENTENCE SUSPENDED ON MEDICAL GROUNDS

Years active: BES collapse in July–August 2014 (Portugal's largest ever banking failure, requiring an €4.9 billion resolution); subsequent criminal proceedings 2014–2026. Institution involved: Banco Espírito Santo (BES), Grupo Espírito Santo (GES), Bank of Portugal, Ministry of Economy. Mechanism: Two separate, fully adjudicated criminal cases: 1. Internal embezzlement/"abuse of trust" — diversion of BES/GES funds by its own chairman. 2. The "EDP case" — bribery of the sitting Minister of Economy in exchange for regulatory favors to Grupo Espírito Santo.

Legal status of Ricardo Salgado — precisely stated:

Case 1 — BES abuse-of-trust ("abuso de confiança"): Sentenced in 2022 to 6 years for three counts; increased to 8 years by the Lisbon Court of Appeal on 24 May 2023. Salgado dropped further appeal. The Constitutional Court rejected the defense's last complaint on 30 October 2025, at which point the 8-year sentence became final ("transitado em julgado"/"definitiva"). Sources: ECO, March 2022; Observador, 24 May 2023; JN — sentence becomes final

Case 2 — "Caso EDP" (bribery of Economy Minister Manuel Pinho): On 6 June 2024, a Lisbon court sentenced Salgado to 6 years and 3 months for bribing then-Economy Minister Manuel Pinho with approximately €4.9 million in exchange for favoring Grupo Espírito Santo's interests. The Lisbon Court of Appeal confirmed this sentence on 11 April 2025; it stands at that level (below the threshold requiring Supreme Court review), making it effectively final for Salgado. Sources: SWI swissinfo.ch, 6 June 2024; Correio da Manhã, 11 April 2025

Combined sentence, no prison time: On 2 June 2026, applying "cúmulo jurídico" (legal aggregation of sentences from both final cases), the court set a combined term of 13 years, but suspended its execution: a forensic exam by the Instituto de Medicina Legal found Salgado, now suffering from Alzheimer's disease, incapable of understanding why he would be imprisoned; the court and the Ministério Público itself argued incarceration would be "contrary to human dignity." Salgado is subject to ongoing semi-annual medical review. This is a suspension of sentence execution, not an acquittal or annulment — the convictions themselves remain final and on his record. Sources: Público, 2 June 2026; RTP, 2 June 2026

Correction to a common misconception: Ricardo Salgado is alive as of this writing (July 2026). No conviction against him has been extinguished by death, and reports of his death should not be relied upon.

Pending, non-final matter: Salgado is also one of 21 co-defendants in the Operação Marquês trial (Section 2.1), charged with 8 counts of money laundering and 3 of active corruption. On 24–25 February 2026, the Lisbon Court of Appeal rejected a defense motion to terminate this prosecution on medical/incapacity grounds, ruling it had no legal basis; the case remains active and unresolved. This charge is not final and is not counted as a conviction in this dossier. Source: Observador, 25 February 2026

Web of connections: the Economy Minister who received the bribes in the EDP case (see Section 2.3) had his own conviction upheld on appeal but has escalated to the Supreme Court and is therefore not yet final; he is covered separately below with his own explicit status.

Sources: ECO, March 2022; Observador, 24 May 2023; Público, 2 June 2026; Correio da Manhã, 11 April 2025

2.3 Caso EDP — Manuel Pinho bribery case — NOT YET FINAL (pending Supreme Court appeal)

Years active: Alleged conduct 2005–2009 (during Pinho's tenure as Minister of Economy); investigation and prosecution 2014–2026. Institution involved: Ministry of Economy; energy utility EDP; Grupo Espírito Santo/BES. Mechanism: Prosecutors alleged Manuel Pinho received approximately €4.9 million from Ricardo Salgado's banking group in exchange for shaping energy-sector regulation favorable to Grupo Espírito Santo interests while serving as Economy Minister.

Legal status: - Manuel Pinho, former Minister of Economy (2005–2009): convicted 6 June 2024 of passive corruption and tax fraud, sentenced to 10 years; confirmed by the Lisbon Court of Appeal on 11 April 2025. Pinho appealed to the Supreme Court (STJ), which admitted the appeal on 25 February 2026 to consider procedural and statute-of-limitations arguments. His conviction is therefore not yet final as of July 2026 and he is not counted as a "convicted" person under this dossier's rule; he is named here only for factual completeness of the case description. Sources: Portugal Resident; CNN Portugal, 9 March 2026 - Alexandra Pinho (Manuel Pinho's wife): sentenced to a suspended term of 4 years 8 months, confirmed by the Court of Appeal 11 April 2025. As a suspended sentence upheld on appeal, this is treated as final; she received no active prison time. Source: Correio da Manhã, 11 April 2025 - Ricardo Salgado's 6-year-3-month sentence in this same case is effectively final (see Section 2.2).

Aftermath: the case is one of the clearest documented instances of alleged high-level political-financial collusion in Portugal, but its lead defendant (the minister himself) has not yet exhausted appeals, illustrating how slowly Portuguese corruption prosecutions move even after a first conviction.

Sources: swissinfo.ch, 6 June 2024; Correio da Manhã, 11 April 2025; CNN Portugal, 9 March 2026

2.4 Processo Face Oculta — FINAL CONVICTIONS

Years active: Corrupt scheme operated through the 2000s; investigated 2008–2009; tried at the Tribunal de Aveiro November 2011–September 2014; appeals concluded 2017–2019. Institution involved: State-linked companies including REN (national grid operator), EDP (energy utility), Portugal Telecom, Caixa Geral de Depósitos (CGD), REFER, Lisnave, Petrogal, Galp, and Empordef. Mechanism: Aveiro-area businessman Manuel Godinho corrupted executives at large state-linked companies to steer public and semi-public contracts (notably scrap/waste-collection contracts) to his firms; a linked offshoot of the investigation uncovered large-scale tax fraud via false invoicing. Thirty-four people and two companies were charged in total.

Amounts: approximately €14 million in tax harm from the false-invoicing scheme; approximately €44 million in false invoices identified; approximately €13 million in civil indemnities ordered against defendants.

2.5 Operação Influencer — António Costa resignation

Years active: Investigation opened around 2023; resignation and immediate aftermath November 2023. Institution involved: Office of the Prime Minister; Ministry of Infrastructure; lithium mining, green hydrogen, and Sines data-center projects. Mechanism: A Supreme Court-led inquiry investigated alleged irregularities in the licensing/authorization of a lithium mining project, a green hydrogen plant, and a data-center project in Sines, prompting 42 coordinated searches on 7 November 2023, including at the Prime Minister's official residence and office.

Legal status: - António Costa, Prime Minister, resigned 7 November 2023, the same day, after learning he was personally the target ("visado") of the Supreme Court inquiry. In his televised resignation address he stated: "Quero dizer, olhos nos olhos aos portugueses, que não me pesa na consciência a prática de qualquer ato ilícito ou sequer de qualquer ato censurável." ("I want to say, eye to eye with the Portuguese people, that no illicit act, or even any reprehensible act, weighs on my conscience.") He explicitly framed the resignation as necessary because "the dignity of the office of prime minister is not compatible with any suspicion over one's integrity." This is a documented resignation over a scandal; no criminal charge or conviction is attributed to Costa in this dossier, consistent with the strict rule — he qualifies here solely on the resignation criterion. Sources: Full resignation speech transcript, RR; NPR, 7 November 2023; Al Jazeera, 7 November 2023 - João Galamba, Minister of Infrastructure, resigned 13 November 2023 after being formally named a suspect ("arguido") on 7 November 2023 in the same investigation. Source: contemporary Portuguese press coverage of the government's collapse, November 2023.

Aftermath: Costa's resignation triggered early legislative elections in March 2024; he subsequently took up the post of President of the European Council. No final conviction has been reported for Costa or Galamba in connection with this inquiry as of July 2026; they are included here on the resignation criterion only.

2.6 Miguel Alves resignation — Secretary of State, "Operação Teia"

Years active: Alleged conduct while mayor of Caminha (prior to 2021); prosecution and resignation 2022 onward. Institution involved: Secretariat of State (central government); Câmara Municipal de Caminha (local government, in his prior role as mayor). Mechanism: Miguel Alves, while serving as a central-government Secretary of State, was formally charged ("acusado") by the Ministério Público with prevaricação (a public-official malfeasance offense) relating to a public contracting irregularity from his earlier tenure as mayor of Caminha.

Legal status: Alves resigned 10 November 2022, the day the formal charge was announced, before any trial took place. In his resignation letter he wrote: "Face à acusação deduzida pelo Ministério Público, e mesmo não tendo conhecimento dos seus termos e pressupostos, entendo não estarem reunidas as condições que permitam a minha permanência no Governo de Portugal." ("Given the charge brought by the Public Prosecutor's Office, and even without knowledge of its terms and grounds, I understand that the conditions for my continued presence in the Government of Portugal are not met.") He also asserted his innocence, saying his conscience was clear. He qualifies for this dossier on the resignation criterion only. He was reported acquitted at first instance in February 2024, with the Ministério Público appealing; that appeal remains pending, so no final judicial outcome (conviction or acquittal) can yet be attributed to him — only the resignation is treated as established fact here. Sources: Visão, 10 November 2022; ECO, 10 November 2022

Note on scope and exclusions

This dossier deliberately does not present José Sócrates or Manuel Pinho as "convicted" individuals, despite the prominence of their cases, because neither has a final conviction as of the July 2026 research date (Sócrates's trial is ongoing with no verdict; Pinho's conviction is under active Supreme Court appeal). It also does not treat Sócrates's 2011 resignation as a corruption-related event, since contemporaneous sources tie it to the sovereign debt crisis and a rejected austerity package, three and a half years before any corruption investigation became public. Ricardo Salgado is presented with the nuance the record demands: two final convictions stand, but the combined sentence's execution has been suspended on medical grounds, which is not the same as an acquittal, pardon, or death-related case termination — and he still faces an unresolved corruption prosecution in the Operação Marquês trial. Where sources conflicted or a detail could not be independently corroborated (for example, Salgado's own appellate path in the EDP case beyond the Court of Appeal), this is flagged rather than resolved by assumption.
