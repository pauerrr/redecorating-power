Factual reference document for Domestic Data Streamers workshop use. Compiled from court records, wire services, and major outlets. Every named individual is included only if they (a) hold a final criminal conviction (upheld on appeal / confirmed by the Tribunal Supremo, not under appeal), or (b) resigned from office over an extensively documented scandal. People who were merely accused, acquitted, or remain under active/unresolved judicial process are referred to only by role, never by name. See the exclusion note at the end of each case and in the closing summary.

1. Country Snapshot

  • Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI) 2025 (Transparency International, published Feb 2026): Spain scores 55/100, ranking 49th of 182 countries — down 3 places year-on-year, and down within the EU from 16th to 17th among member states. transparency.org/en/countries/spain
  • CPI trend: Spain's score has drifted down from the high 60s in the mid-2010s to the mid-50s range by 2024–2025, reflecting sustained perception of unresolved institutional weaknesses rather than any single new scandal. transparency.org/en/cpi/2025
  • Eurobarometer, Special Eurobarometer 534 "Citizens' attitudes towards corruption in the EU" (2023): across the EU, 70% of citizens said corruption is widespread in their own country (survey of 26,404 citizens, fieldwork March–May 2023); Spain was among the member states where over 90% of respondents said it is never acceptable to offer money or a favor to a public official in exchange for a service — indicating strong public rejection of bribery even as perceived prevalence remains high. home-affairs.ec.europa.eu, July 2023 / europa.eu/eurobarometer/surveys/detail/2968
  • Estimated cost of corruption: A widely cited European Parliament–commissioned analysis (building on the EPRS "Cost of Non-Europe" corruption studies) put Spain's annual loss to corruption at approximately €90 billion a year, roughly 8% of GDP — the fourth-highest absolute loss among EU member states. euroweeklynews.com, Dec 2018, citing EP figures; EU-wide corruption cost estimates of up to €990 billion/year appear in the RAND Europe study for the European Parliament. rand.org / europarl.europa.eu/thinktank, 2023
  • GRECO (Council of Europe, Group of States against Corruption): In its Fourth Evaluation Round follow-up (addendum to the Second Compliance Report, concluded 2024), GRECO found Spain had fully implemented 7 of its recommendations on corruption prevention among MPs, judges and prosecutors, partly implemented 3, and had made no progress on the recommendation to reform the appointment system of the General Council of the Judiciary (CGPJ) — the body that selects judges — since its 2022 review. GRECO formally closed the Fourth Round compliance procedure for Spain at that point despite the outstanding CGPJ item. coe.int/en/web/greco

2. Major Cases

2.1 Gürtel Case (with the Bárcenas Papers / PP "Caja B" slush fund)

Years active: approximately 1999–2009 (network); the Bárcenas parallel-accounting ledgers cover 1990–2009. Institution involved: Partido Popular (PP) at national and regional (Madrid, Valencia) level; regional and municipal public contracting bodies. Mechanism: A business network led by Francisco Correa won public contracts (events, fairs, municipal services) through bid-rigging and kickbacks; part of the profits were funneled back to PP officials and used to illegally finance party campaigns via a secret parallel accounting ledger ("Caja B") kept by the party treasurer, alongside personal enrichment and cash payments to senior party figures, including then—Prime Minister Mariano Rajoy, according to the Audiencia Nacional's findings. Amounts: The Audiencia Nacional's May 2018 ruling (Gürtel I, "Fitur" phase) found illegal financing and unlawful enrichment; separately, Bárcenas was found to have hidden over €6 million from tax authorities (2003, 2006, 2007) and held undeclared Swiss accounts around €38–48 million reported in press coverage of the leaked "papeles de Bárcenas."

Key people — final legal status:

Person Status
Francisco Correa (ringleader) Convicted 2018 by the Audiencia Nacional; sentence upheld with minor adjustments by the Tribunal Supremo, October 2020. Total sentence 51 years and 11 months in prison (combined across Gürtel I "Fitur" phase counts of bribery, influence peddling, money laundering, tax fraud, document forgery).
Pablo Crespo (Correa's second-in-command) Convicted 2018, sentence upheld by Tribunal Supremo October 2020: 37 years and 6 months in prison.
Luis Bárcenas (former PP national treasurer) Convicted 2018 by the Audiencia Nacional (Gürtel/"cash box" trial) on money laundering, tax fraud and related counts; sentence upheld with reductions by the Tribunal Supremo, October 2020, at roughly 29 years cumulative (approx. 12 years effective under Spanish accumulation rules), plus fines exceeding €44 million. In a related, separate proceeding over black-market cash payments for renovation works at PP's Madrid headquarters, the Tribunal Supremo confirmed his conviction in November 2024, reducing that specific sentence to 8 months. He was granted conditional release in December 2024 after serving two-thirds of his custodial sentence.
Rosalía Iglesias (Bárcenas's wife) Convicted 2018, sentence upheld by Tribunal Supremo October 2020: 15 years and 1 month in prison for money laundering and tax fraud.
Álvaro Pérez, "El Bigotes" (Orange Market) Convicted by the Audiencia Nacional for the Valencia regional financing scheme (2007–2008 campaigns); sentence of 6 years and 9 months confirmed on appeal.
Ana Mato (former Minister of Health) Resigned from the government on 26 November 2014 after a judge found she had personally benefited ("partícipe a título lucrativo," a civil, not criminal, finding) from gifts and trips paid for by the Gürtel network via her then-husband; she paid a €27,857 fine as a civil beneficiary. She was never criminally charged or convicted herself.
Partido Popular (as legal entity) Held civilly liable by the Audiencia Nacional (2018) and confirmed by the Tribunal Supremo (October 2020) as an unlawful "beneficiario a título lucrativo" (lucrative participant), ordered to repay €133,628 and €111,864 for illegally financed campaign events in Majadahonda and Pozuelo; the court explicitly did not find the party itself guilty of a criminal offense as an institution.

Explicitly excluded: Mariano Rajoy testified as a witness in the Gürtel trial in July 2017 — the first sitting Spanish prime minister to testify in a criminal case — and the court's ruling stated his testimony denying knowledge of the parallel accounting was "not sufficiently credible," but he was never charged or convicted of any crime and did not resign over Gürtel (he lost office in June 2018 via a parliamentary no-confidence vote, a constitutional political mechanism, not a personal resignation). He is therefore excluded from the named list above and referred to here only in his role as head of government. Francisco Camps, former president of the Valencia region, was acquitted in the Valencia branch of the case and is excluded by name.

Aftermath: The Audiencia Nacional's 24 May 2018 Gürtel I ruling — finding the PP had operated an illegal parallel financing structure since at least 1989/1990 — triggered a motion of no confidence in Congress days later; Rajoy lost the vote on 1 June 2018 and Pedro Sánchez became prime minister, marking the first successful no-confidence motion in Spanish democratic history. The case prompted reforms to party-financing transparency law and put PP under sustained "caja B" scrutiny for years afterward.

2.2 ERE Case (Andalusia Employment Subsidy Fraud)

Years active: approximately 2000–2011. Institution involved: Junta de Andalucía (regional government), then governed continuously by PSOE. Mechanism: A regional budget line ("partida 31L") was used to distribute early-retirement and business-crisis subsidies ("ayudas sociolaborales") to workers of failing companies and consultancy fees, without the standard public subsidy procedures, competitive process, or effective auditing — allowing diversion of public funds, including to individuals and firms not entitled to aid. Amounts: The original Seville court judgment (2019) quantified irregularly distributed funds at approximately €680 million for 2000–2009 (of which €606.2 million was labor aid and €73.8 million direct company aid); subsequent reporting/audits suggest the wider irregular envelope (1999–2011) could exceed €1 billion, though only the €680 million figure was judicially established in the original ruling.

Critical legal-status update: In 2019, the Seville Provincial Court convicted a group of former Andalusian regional officials, including former regional presidents José Antonio Griñán and Manuel Chaves, of prevarication and (for Griñán) embezzlement; the Tribunal Supremo confirmed these convictions on appeal in 2022. However, on 16 July 2024 Spain's Constitutional Court (Tribunal Constitucional) annulled the embezzlement conviction against Griñán entirely and ordered the Seville court to substantially reduce Chaves's disqualification sentence, ruling that the drafting and approval of the annual budget line by regional officials constituted ordinary political acts that should not have been criminalized. As of this writing, the Seville Provincial Court has empaneled a new bench to issue a revised sentence, and no final, standing criminal conviction currently exists against either man for the ERE case.

Consequence for this dossier: Because their convictions have been judicially annulled and a final resentencing is still pending, neither José Antonio Griñán nor Manuel Chaves can be listed as finally convicted under the strict rule applied here. Chaves additionally never resigned over the case — asked repeatedly to step down between 2011 and 2015, he stated publicly, "Llevan 20 años pidiendo mi dimisión. No he sido acusado, ni imputado, ni juzgado" ("They've been asking for my resignation for 20 years. I haven't been accused, charged, or tried") — so he does not meet the resignation criterion either. Both are therefore excluded by name from the people index; the case is described here by mechanism and institutional role only. Other regional officials named in reporting (e.g., former finance councillor and former head of the regional development agency IDEA) also had their sentences affected by the same Constitutional Court ruling and are excluded for the same reason.

Aftermath: The ERE case became shorthand in Spanish politics for regional-government patronage and lax subsidy controls; it damaged the PSOE's standing in its historic Andalusian stronghold and contributed to the PP's first-ever regional election win there in 2018, ending 36 years of PSOE government in Andalusia. It also prompted tightened Andalusian subsidy-control and audit legislation.

Sources: - Wikipedia (Spanish) — Caso ERE en Andalucía - El Diario — Tribunal Constitucional annuls Griñán/Chaves convictions, July 2024 - Infobae — Constitutional Court annuls convictions, 16 July 2024 - El Español — New Seville tribunal empaneled for resentencing, Nov 2024

2.3 Púnica Case (Madrid Regional Public Contracting Network)

Years active: approximately 2003–2014 (multiple sub-schemes, prosecuted in separate "piezas" or case-branches). Institution involved: Regional government of the Community of Madrid (PP-led), plus several municipal governments across party lines. Mechanism: A network centered on a former Madrid regional minister allegedly channeled public contracts — including energy-efficiency retrofitting contracts for municipal buildings awarded to the firm Cofely, and other public works and services — through bid manipulation in exchange for bribes and illegal party financing, alongside alleged use of shell companies and a "reputation management" operation to place favorable/negative press coverage of public figures. Amounts: The energy-contracts branch alone ("Cofely piece," Audiencia Nacional ruling, December 2025) covered public contracts described as the largest single monetary volume in the case; other Púnica sub-cases remain in investigation or trial.

Legal-status note — this case is largely unresolved: Púnica is composed of at least twelve separate judicial "pieces," and as of publicly available reporting, only one final Supreme Court–confirmed individual conviction exists to date across the entire twelve-year investigation: former Madrid regional minister Francisco Granados received a 2-year prison sentence, confirmed by the Tribunal Supremo in 2019, for leaking details of the police investigation to a suspect (an obstruction-type offense) — not for the core bid-rigging/kickback allegations, which remain pending trial against him as of 2026. The most economically significant branch (the "Cofely" energy-contracts piece) produced an Audiencia Nacional first-instance ruling in December 2025 convicting 29 people, including six former Madrid-region mayors, with sentences up to 8 years and 2 months — but this ruling is recent and, per available reporting, its finality/appeal status had not been resolved at time of writing.

Consequence for this dossier: Because the substantive Púnica corruption convictions are either not yet final (Cofely piece, Dec 2025, likely under appeal) or not yet tried (the main "pieza 8" case against the network's alleged organizers was still pending trial as of mid-2026), individuals are not named here except for Francisco Granados's narrow, confirmed 2019 conviction for the investigation leak — which is noted for context but is not itself a corruption/kickback conviction. Other defendants are referred to only by role (e.g., "former mayors," "a former regional minister," "a private-sector intermediary").

Aftermath: Púnica triggered a wide internal crisis in the Madrid PP, prompted the resignation of several municipal officials from public roles (without individual criminal resolution to date), and fed into a broader 2014–2015 wave of Spanish corruption scandals (alongside Gürtel and the Bárcenas revelations) that contributed to the fragmentation of Spain's two-party system and the rise of new political parties (Podemos, Ciudadanos) in the 2015–2016 general elections.

Sources: - Wikipedia (Spanish) — Operación Púnica - Infobae — Audiencia Nacional sentences 29 people in Cofely piece, Dec 2025 - Poder Judicial (CGPJ) — Supreme Court confirms Granados's 2-year sentence for leak - Diari de Tarragona — "Twelve pieces, only one final conviction in twelve years"

2.4 Nóos Case (Iñaki Urdangarin / Instituto Nóos)

Years active: approximately 2004–2006. Institution involved: Instituto Nóos, a nonprofit run by Iñaki Urdangarin (brother-in-law of King Felipe VI, husband of Infanta Cristina), in contract with the regional governments of the Balearic Islands and Valencia. Mechanism: Nóos was awarded public contracts and subsidies to organize sports/tourism promotion events; investigators found the foundation systematically overbilled for these events and diverted profits through a private company (Aizoon) to fund the Urdangarin-Torres families' personal expenses, including home renovation, travel and consumer purchases. Amounts: Approximately €6 million in public funds were found to have been improperly obtained via inflated invoicing across the Balearic and Valencian contracts; a separate, specific finding involved roughly €445,000 in Balearic government contracts for events never fully delivered.

Key people — final legal status:

Person Status
Iñaki Urdangarin Convicted by the Palma de Mallorca Provincial Court, 17 February 2017; sentence confirmed (with a 5-month reduction) by the Tribunal Supremo, 12 June 2018 — final sentence 5 years and 10 months in prison, plus tax-fraud, embezzlement, influence-peddling and fraud counts and financial restitution obligations. He entered prison in 2018 and was the first member of the extended Spanish royal family to receive a final criminal conviction. He and his wife were stripped of the title Duke/Duchess of Palma de Mallorca by King Felipe VI in June 2015, ahead of the final verdict.
Diego Torres (Urdangarin's business partner, Nóos co-director) Convicted by the Palma court (initially 8 years 6 months); sentence reduced and made final by the Tribunal Supremo, confirmed 8 June 2018, at 5 years and 8 months in prison.

Explicitly excluded: Infanta Cristina de Borbón (Urdangarin's wife, sister of King Felipe VI) was tried alongside her husband on tax-fraud-related charges but was acquitted by the Palma court on 17 February 2017; she held no public office and never resigned any position. She is excluded by name from any convicted/resigned list and referred to only as "a member of the royal family" where contextually necessary. Ana María Tejeiro (Diego Torres's wife) was also acquitted and is excluded.

Aftermath: The case caused sustained reputational damage to the Spanish monarchy during the final years of King Juan Carlos I's reign and the early reign of Felipe VI; it contributed to the drafting of a 2014 law (following Juan Carlos I's abdication) formalizing scrutiny of royal family finances, and to the 2020 decision by Juan Carlos I to relocate abroad amid separate, unrelated financial-disclosure controversies.

Sources: - Poder Judicial (CGPJ) — Supreme Court sentences Urdangarin to 5 years 10 months - France 24 — Spanish court clears Princess Cristina, jails husband, Feb 2017 - Público — Supreme Court rejects appeal to annul final Urdangarin sentence - Wikipedia — Nóos case

2.5 Palma Arena Case (Balearic Islands)

Years active: approximately 2004–2007 (public works and PR contracts related to the Palma Arena velodrome and related regional government spending). Institution involved: Balearic Islands regional government, under president Jaume Matas. Mechanism: The umbrella investigation covered cost overruns on the Palma Arena velodrome's construction and a series of separate contracts in which regional government funds were allegedly funneled to political allies and PR/communications contractors in exchange for favorable treatment, including a journalist paid via a public contract to ghostwrite speeches and advise the president, and a subsidized news agency. Amounts: The influence-peddling count for which Matas's final conviction stands involved roughly €200,000 in public payments to a speechwriting/advisory contractor and a separate €450,000 subsidy to a news agency; the wider Palma Arena venue itself ran tens of millions of euros over its original budget, though those cost-overrun allegations did not result in his final personal conviction.

Key person — final legal status:

Person Status
Jaume Matas (former president of the Balearic Islands, former government minister) Convicted on multiple counts by the Palma de Mallorca provincial court (initial sentence around 6 years across several offenses); on final appeal, the Tribunal Supremo, October 2017, overturned or reduced most counts and confirmed only a single offense — trafficking in influence — with a final sentence of 9 months and 1 day in prison, below the two-year threshold that in Spain typically allows a first-time offender to avoid actually entering custody.

Aftermath: The case was one of the first major Spanish regional-corruption prosecutions of a sitting-era political leader to reach a final conviction (2017), predating the larger Gürtel and ERE final rulings; it contributed to the erosion of PP support in the Balearic Islands through the 2010s and to broader public debate over regional government oversight of public relations and communications spending.

Sources: - Poder Judicial (CGPJ) — Supreme Court convicts Matas of influence trafficking, 9 months - El Español — Matas avoids prison over Palma Arena contracts, Oct 2017 - Wikipedia — Jaume Matas
